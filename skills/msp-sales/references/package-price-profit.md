## The Central Thesis: Value-Based Pricing

The most common MSP pricing mistake is **cost-plus pricing**: add up your tool costs, multiply
by a margin, call it your price. This commoditizes your service and anchors clients on cost
rather than value.

**Value-based pricing** asks a different question: *What is a stable, secure, responsive IT
environment worth to this business?* The answer is almost always far more than the cost of your
tools.

**In practice:**
- Quantify the cost of downtime for the prospect: "How much revenue do you lose per hour if
  your team can't work?"
- Quantify the cost of a breach or data loss event
- Frame your price against that number: $X/month vs. $50,000+ ransomware recovery

The MSP who sells on value wins on price. The MSP who sells on features competes on price.

## The Problem With Most MSP Pricing Models

This methodology identifies three ways MSP pricing models fail:

**1. Under-pricing out of fear.** Most MSPs undercharge because they're afraid of losing the
deal. The result: they win clients, but the clients are unprofitable. Low-priced clients demand
the most support, generate the least profit, and prevent you from taking on better-fit clients.

**2. Complexity that confuses buyers.** Too many plans, too many add-ons, too many exceptions.
Confused prospects don't buy, they stall.

**3. Paralysis by analysis.** "Probably everyone in the MSP space is a perfectionist or recovering
perfectionist." MSPs spend months building the perfect pricing structure and never launch.
This methodology's advice: **Done is better than perfect.** Get something out, learn from
clients in the field, iterate.

## The Tiered Plan Framework (Good / Better / Best)

This methodology recommends 2-3 service tiers as the sweet spot. More than 3 creates decision paralysis
for prospects; fewer than 2 removes the upsell path.

**Naming conventions (keep it simple):**
- Good / Better / Best
- Essentials / Standard / Premium
- Silver / Gold / Platinum
- Foundation / Business / Enterprise

Avoid abstract names that require explanation. If a prospect has to ask "what does 'Apex' mean?"
you've already created friction.

**What differentiates tiers (recommended):**
- Included services (e.g., backup included at mid-tier; advanced security at top tier)
- Coverage scope (e.g., workstations only vs. workstations + servers + network)
- Proactivity level (e.g., reactive helpdesk vs. proactive maintenance and patching)
- Software inclusions (e.g., productivity suite licenses bundled in top tier)

**What should NOT differentiate tiers:**
- Response times. Don't promise 4-hour response on Basic and 1-hour on Premium.
  This sends the wrong message ("we'll treat some clients as second-class") and creates
  operational complexity. All clients should receive your best response time. That's your
  brand promise.

**The anchor effect:** Always present 3 tiers. Most prospects will choose the middle option.
Your bottom tier exists to make the middle look reasonable; your top tier exists to capture
the clients who want everything and will pay for it.

## Standardizing Your Stack

One of the strongest operational points in this methodology: **your stack is your business.**

Every time a client demands you use their preferred tools instead of yours, you're taking on
custom configuration work that:
- Can't be templated or automated
- Creates unique support challenges
- Makes offboarding messy
- Prevents you from scaling

**The rule:** You control the tools. You present your stack as part of the value proposition.

**How to explain it in sales:**
> "We've built our entire operation around a specific set of tools, tools we know inside and
> out and have optimized for your type of business. When we manage 50 clients using the same
> platform, we get very good at it very fast. If we use your custom setup, you're getting
> a generic version of us. You deserve the optimized version."

**In practice:**
- Define your standard stack before you take on clients
- Include stack standardization in your onboarding agreement
- Charge a setup/migration fee to bring clients onto your stack. Never absorb that cost

## "Per User 2.0": Beyond Simple Per-User Pricing

Standard per-user pricing (same price for every seat) ignores the reality that not all users
cost the same to support. This methodology advocates for a weighted approach:

**User types in a typical SMB environment:**
- **Standard user** (workstation + productivity suite licensing): your base rate
- **Power user** (multiple monitors, specialized software, higher support volume): higher rate
- **Remote/light user** (email-only, no managed workstation): lower rate
- **Server**: separate flat rate per server
- **Network equipment**: separate flat rate per device

This maps directly to the per-device/per-seat model already in use. The key is pricing each
category correctly rather than averaging everything into one number.

**The risk of flat-average pricing:** You overprice simple users (chasing away easy wins) and
underprice complex users (losing money on your hardest accounts).

## Marketing Principles for MSPs (US-Applicable)

This methodology is consistent that the #1 struggle for MSPs is lead generation, and that most MSPs
avoid marketing because they don't understand the structure.

**Marketing is a long game.** Most MSP deals close 6-18 months after first contact. The MSPs
who win are the ones who stayed visible while competitors went silent.

### What actually works for US MSP marketing:

**1. Referral programs: still the highest-converting channel**
- Most MSP new business comes from referrals, yet most MSPs have no formal referral program
- Build one: give existing clients an easy way to refer, acknowledge and reward referrals
- Referral partners: accountants, attorneys, insurance brokers (especially cyber insurance
  brokers), commercial real estate agents, banks, all of which regularly work with SMBs

**2. Niche marketing (when you're ready)**
- Targeting a vertical (accountants, law firms, medical practices) dramatically improves
  marketing ROI: same message, same content, smaller audience, higher relevance
- Narrowing the niche pours fuel on your results: the same effort produces sharper messaging
  and a higher-converting audience.
- US startup recommendation: Don't niche until you have 10+ clients. Build general pipeline
  first, then double down on the vertical you're winning in.
- **Exclude from US strategy:** Australian-specific vertical regulations or compliance
  requirements (e.g., My Health Record, AU Privacy Act)

**3. Content that educates, not pitches**
- Create a **Buyer's Guide**: "What to Look for in an IT Provider" or "10 Questions to Ask
  Before Hiring an IT Company." This builds trust, positions you as an authority, and disqualifies
  bad-fit prospects before they waste your time.
- Cybersecurity guides perform well: "Is Your Business Cyber Insurance-Ready?" "5 Signs You're
  a Ransomware Target"
- Simple checklists and infographics outperform long-form for SMB audiences

**4. Short video content**
- Short, authentic videos (30-90 seconds) on a professional social network and email perform
  strongly for MSPs
- Topics: quick tips, myth-busting, behind-the-scenes, client stories
- You don't need production quality: a phone, good lighting, and a clear point is enough
- **US channels to focus on:** a professional social network (B2B decision-makers), email
  newsletters, and a local business listing profile (for local search)

**5. Local Business Listing Profile (local SEO)**
- Many SMBs searching for IT support use "IT support near me" or "managed IT [city]"
- A complete, review-rich local business listing profile is free and generates inbound leads
- Ask every happy client for an online review: this is one of the highest-leverage free
  activities for a startup MSP

## The "Trusted Advisor" Shift

The transformation from introverted technician to trusted seller is a core theme in this
methodology's teaching. The shift is from:

| Technician Mindset | Trusted Advisor Mindset |
|-------------------|------------------------|
| "Let me explain the technology" | "Let me understand your business problem" |
| Leads with features | Leads with questions |
| Defends the price | Justifies the value |
| Responds to requests | Anticipates needs |
| Vendor relationship | Partner relationship |

**The key behavior shift:** Stop pitching and start diagnosing. A doctor doesn't lead with
"here's a great medication." They ask questions first, then prescribe. MSPs who lead with
services lose to MSPs who lead with understanding.

**Practical application:**
- In every prospect meeting, spend the first 60% asking questions (see discovery-questions.md)
- Repeat back what you heard before presenting solutions
- Reference their specific situation in your proposal, not a generic template
- Follow up after onboarding with "how are things going?" calls, not just support tickets

## Pricing Confidence: The Mindset Issue

This methodology addresses what it calls the "money mindset" problem directly: a scarcity
mindset around money, often rooted in personal upbringing, that can sabotage an owner's ability
to sell at the right price.

**Common pricing confidence failures:**
- Discounting before the prospect even asks
- Apologizing for your price ("I know it might seem like a lot, but...")
- Quoting low to "get in the door" and raise prices later (rarely works)
- Matching competitor pricing without knowing your own costs

**The fix:**
- Know your numbers before every proposal: what does it cost you to deliver this service?
  What margin do you need to be profitable and grow?
- Present price confidently, then stop talking. Silence after quoting is normal.
- "Our price is $X per month. That covers everything we discussed." Full stop.
- If they push back, ask questions. Don't immediately discount

**US market context (industry data):** US SMBs pay $75-$200+ per user per month for fully
managed services, depending on scope and market. Entry-level managed services (helpdesk +
monitoring) can start at $50-$75/user. Security-heavy or compliance-driven packages command
$150-$300+/user. Startup MSPs should resist the urge to undercut: it attracts the wrong clients.
