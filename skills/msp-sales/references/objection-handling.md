# Objection Handling Guide

**Rule:** Never argue. Acknowledge -> Empathize -> Reframe -> Ask.

The formula: "I hear you. A lot of people feel that way. Here's what they usually find. Can I ask..."

**Format note:** These talk tracks are written for live calls. For written replies (email, social
message, text), convert to linear prose: drop the [wait for answer] beats and bracketed stage
directions, merge the back-and-forth into one flowing message, and keep it to one screen.

---

## Objection 1: "It's too expensive" / "We can't afford it"

**What they actually mean:** "I don't see enough value yet" or "I don't know what I'm comparing this to."

**What NOT to do:** Immediately discount, defend the price, or justify with a feature list.

**Response:**
> "Totally fair, that's the first thing everyone looks at. Can I ask what you're spending
> on IT right now?"

*[Wait for answer. Often they don't actually know]*

> "That's what I find most of the time. Most businesses are spending more than they realize,
> and it's usually unpredictable. Bills when things break, time your team spends dealing with
> issues, that kind of thing. Our engagements start at $1,000 a month, and where yours would
> land depends on what we find in discovery: how many people, devices, and locations we'd be
> taking care of. Whatever the number is, it replaces all of that with one flat fee. No
> surprises."

(Do not invent market spend ranges or benchmark figures. The $1,000/month minimum is an example
default; see the Setup Decisions section of SKILL.md to set your own, and this is the only
number that may be quoted before msp-pricing produces the real one.)

*If they're still hesitant:*
> "What I'd really want to understand is: what would it cost you if you had a serious IT problem
> and you were on your own? A day of downtime, a data loss situation. That's really what we're
> talking about replacing."

**Key reframe:** Shift from price comparison to risk comparison.

## Objection 2: "We already have someone who handles IT" / "We have a guy"

**What they actually mean:** "We're loyal to someone we know" or "change feels risky."

**What NOT to do:** Trash the current provider. It'll backfire.

**Response:**
> "That's great. Having someone you trust matters a lot. Can I ask, is this a full-time
> employee, or someone you bring in when needed?"

*[If it's a "guy they call"]:*
> "That works fine when things are small. A lot of our clients started exactly that way. What
> usually happens is the business grows, the needs get more complex, and that one person either
> can't keep up or isn't available when something urgent happens. Is that ever been a concern?"

*[If it's another MSP]:*
> "How's that relationship going? Are you happy with them?"

*[If unhappy]:* "What's not working?"

*[If happy]:* "Good to know. I'll be honest: if you're truly happy, I'm not going to try to
convince you to change. But if there's ever a moment where you're wondering if you're getting
full value, I'd love to be the second opinion. Can I stay in touch?"

**Key reframe:** Make the conversation about gaps and growth, not about replacing loyalty.

### Variant: "My nephew handles our IT" plus price (the stacked objection)

Family loyalty stacked on price is the hardest version of "we have a guy." Never disparage the
nephew. He may be genuinely good, he probably got you the meeting warm, and he will definitely
be at Thanksgiving. Respect the relationship first, then reframe around coverage and
accountability, not skill.

**Response:**
> "Honestly, that's great. Family who steps up like that is worth a lot, and I'm not here to
> talk you out of it."

> "Can I ask a couple of practical questions? What happens when he's on vacation, or at his
> day job, and something goes down at 10 a.m. on a Tuesday? And if he ever steps back, does
> anyone else know how everything is set up?"

*[Wait for answer]*

> "That's usually the gap. It's not about how good he is; it's about coverage and
> accountability. We're available during business hours every day, our response commitments
> are in writing, and everything about your setup gets documented so no single person is a
> point of failure. Plenty of our clients keep a family helper in the loop; we just carry
> the weight."

*[On the price half of the stack]:*
> "And I get it: he's probably a lot cheaper, maybe free. The honest comparison isn't his
> rate against ours. It's what an outage or a security problem costs you while you wait for
> someone who has another life to get to it."

**The graceful exit:** If loyalty wins, let it win. "It sounds like that arrangement really
matters to you, and I respect it. If anything changes, or if he'd ever like a backup, my door
is open." Log the deal as Closed Lost with the reason, set a long-cycle follow-up, and leave
warm. Family arrangements end on their own schedule; be the first call when this one does.

**Key reframe:** Coverage and accountability, never competence. The nephew is not the problem;
the single point of failure is.

## Objection 3: "We already have an MSP"

**What they actually mean:** One of three things: "Don't waste my time," "I'm not shopping," or
"I'm not thrilled but I'm not ready to switch yet."

**What NOT to do:** Launch into a pitch for why you're better. You don't know enough yet.

**Response:**
> "No problem at all. How long have you been with them?"

*[Listen. Then:]*
> "What's working well?"

*[Let them answer. Then:]*
> "Is there anything you wish was different?"

*[If they mention anything, even minor]:*
> "That's worth exploring. A lot of MSPs do the basics fine, but there's usually something
> (response time, communication, proactive advice) where there's room for more. What does
> that feel like in your case?"

*[If everything is genuinely great]:*
> "I appreciate that. I won't push. The one thing I'd ask is: how long since you've actually
> benchmarked what you're getting vs. what's available? Things change. If you're ever curious,
> I'd be happy to do a no-pressure comparison. Mind if I follow up in a few months?"

**Key reframe:** Plant a seed. Most people leave MSPs over accumulated frustration, not single incidents.

### The Displacement Play (incumbent MSP)

When the incumbent is real and the prospect is a genuine fit, run the long game instead of a
head-to-head pitch:

1. **Plant the second-opinion audit.** "Would a no-pressure outside look at what you're getting
   be useful? Worst case, you confirm you're in good hands." Frame it as a benchmark, not a
   takeover attempt.
2. **Never trash the incumbent.** If you bad-mouth them, you teach the prospect exactly how
   you'll talk about them one day. Praise what's working; let the gaps speak for themselves.
3. **Capture the contract end date.** "So I don't bug you at the wrong time, when does your
   current agreement come up for renewal?" Log the date on the deal in the pipeline.
4. **Time outreach to 90 days before renewal.** That's when notice windows and renewal decisions
   are live. Set the follow-up task the day you learn the date, and stay lightly present in
   between with useful content, not pitches.

## Objection 4: "We're too small for something like this"

**What they actually mean:** "IT support feels like a big-company thing" or "I assume it's not for us."

**What NOT to do:** Immediately list services. They need to believe they're worth protecting first.

**Qualify before you sell past this one:** engagements start at $1,000 a month, and under 10
employees is a red light in the ICP (see ideal-customer-profile.md). If they can't clear the
minimum or the size floor, refer them out gracefully instead of winning a bad-fit deal.

**Response:**
> "You'd be surprised. Actually, small businesses are the most common targets for cyberattacks.
> Hackers know small companies have less protection. The question isn't really size, it's: what
> would happen to your business if your systems went down for a day or two, or if client data
> got compromised?"

*[Let them answer]*

> "That's the real math. And honestly, our smallest clients are sometimes our most appreciative,
> because they get the same level of attention as a 100-person company, but they're not
> competing for their IT team's time. How many people do you have?"

*[Then let the conversation flow naturally into discovery]*

**Key reframe:** Smallness is a risk factor, not an exemption.

## Objection 5: "I need to think about it" / "I'll get back to you"

**What they actually mean:** One of three things: "I'm not convinced yet," "I need approval from
someone else," or "I'm talking to other providers."

**What NOT to do:** Say "sounds good, I'll follow up in a few weeks." That's a deal killer.

**Response:**
> "Of course. This isn't something to rush. Can I ask, is there a specific part of what we
> discussed that you'd want to think through more? I want to make sure I've given you what
> you need to make a good decision."

*[Listen for the real objection hiding underneath]*

*[If they mention price]:* -> Go to Objection 1
*[If they mention needing to talk to a partner/spouse/board]:*

> "Totally makes sense. Is there anything that would make that conversation easier? I'm happy
> to put together a summary, or even join a quick call to answer their questions directly."

*[Set a specific follow-up]:*
> "Let's do this: I'll give you until [specific date]. If it's not the right fit, no hard
> feelings at all, just let me know. But I'd rather not leave things open-ended. Does [date]
> work to reconnect?"

**Key reframe:** Turn "think about it" into a conversation about what's actually in the way.

---

## Objection 6: "What if we need to cancel?" / "What's the contract length?"

**What they actually mean:** "I'm afraid of being locked in with someone bad."

**This is actually a buying signal: they're thinking about signing.**

**Response:**
> "Totally fair question. We do have [your actual terms here]. Here's how I think about it:
> if we're doing our job, you'll never want to leave. And if we're not, you shouldn't be
> locked in. We'd rather earn your business every month than hold you hostage."

*[Briefly explain your actual exit terms. Be honest and confident about them]*

> "Does the term length change whether this makes sense for you, or is there something else
> underneath that concern?"

**Key reframe:** Confidence in your own service. Transparency about terms signals trustworthiness.

## Quick Reference: Objection to Strategy

| Objection | Core Issue | First Move |
|-----------|-----------|------------|
| Too expensive | Value gap | Ask what they're spending now |
| Have a guy | Loyalty / change risk | Ask how it's going; look for gaps |
| Nephew plus price (stacked) | Family loyalty and value gap | Respect first; reframe to coverage and accountability |
| Have an MSP | "Don't bother me" | Ask what's working; plant the second-opinion audit; log the renewal date |
| Too small | Misperception | Reframe smallness as vulnerability |
| Need to think | Hidden objection | Ask what part needs more thought |
| Contract concerns | Fear of lock-in | Buying signal: be transparent, confident |
