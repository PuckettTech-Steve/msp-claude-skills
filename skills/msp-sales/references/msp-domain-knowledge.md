# MSP Domain Knowledge

Source: established MSP industry sales literature and general MSP industry blog research.

## The Core Mindset

> "The MSP business isn't won by those with the greatest technical acumen, it's won by those with
> the greatest sales acumen."

Most MSPs are started by technically excellent people who assume their skills will attract clients.
They're wrong. The business is won through relationships, trust, and the ability to communicate
value to non-technical people. Technicians become MSP owners. Sales-minded operators become
*successful* MSP owners.

**What this means for your team:**
- Every technician is a potential referral source: they should be trained to spot sales opportunities
- Founder involvement in early sales is essential; your credibility closes deals
- The pipeline should always be full, even when you're busy

## The Three Billing Models

Understanding these helps you position against competitors and explain your model's advantages.

### 1. Hourly / Break-Fix
- Client calls when something breaks; you charge by the hour
- **Problem:** Perverse incentives: the more things break, the more you make
- **Client experience:** Unpredictable bills, no relationship, always reactive
- **Your position:** "You deserve an IT partner who's motivated to keep things running, not to fix
  them after they fail."

### 2. Block-Hour Contracts
- Client pre-buys a block of hours (e.g., 10 hours/month); unused hours sometimes roll over
- **Problem:** Still hour-based; clients worry about "burning hours"; you still get paid to fix
- **Client experience:** Better than break-fix, but still adversarial on billing
- **Your position:** "Block hours still mean you're paying for problems. We charge for prevention."

### 3. All-You-Can-Eat (AYCE) Managed Services: Your Model
- Flat monthly fee per device/seat; covers everything in scope
- **Why it wins:** Your incentives align with the client's: if things break constantly, it costs
  *you* time and money, so you're motivated to build stable, reliable environments
- **Client experience:** Predictable costs, proactive support, true partnership
- **Selling point:** "One flat monthly number. No surprise invoices. We succeed when your IT
  just works."

## Why Break-Fix Still Matters (Even for a Managed Services Provider)

Don't dismiss break-fix work. Use it as a pipeline mechanism:

- Many SMBs have never had a managed services provider. They start by calling someone when
  something breaks. That's your foot in the door.
- **Project work as a lure:** A server migration, new computer setup, or Wi-Fi upgrade gives
  you access to the business and a chance to demonstrate your capabilities.
- **Conversion strategy:** After completing a project, say: "We can keep doing this ad-hoc, or
  we can put you on our managed plan so you never have to call us in a panic again. Same price
  as what you've been spending, but now it's predictable."
- Never turn away break-fix work if the client is a good managed services candidate. You're
  not charging less. You're investing in a future contract.

## The One Metric That Matters: Labor Revenue per FTE

Most MSPs track the wrong things (total revenue, ticket counts, uptime percentages). The only
number that determines whether your MSP is healthy is:

**Labor Revenue per FTE = Total Annual Revenue (recurring plus project) / Number of Full-Time
Employees**

- This tells you how efficiently your team is generating revenue
- Industry benchmark: **$150,000-$200,000+ per FTE per year** for a healthy MSP
- Below $100,000/FTE: You have a pricing or efficiency problem
- Growing this number is the primary financial goal, through better pricing, automation, or
  moving clients up-stack

**Use in sales conversations:** When prospects push back on price, remind yourself (and your
team) that underpriced contracts drag down this metric for every employee.

## The QBR Problem (and What to Do Instead)

Quarterly Business Reviews (QBRs) are standard MSP practice: quarterly meeting to review
performance, discuss roadmap, justify your existence. The problem: clients dread them, rarely
show up engaged, and they feel like a retention exercise rather than a real relationship.

**An alternative approach: Be a true business partner**

Instead of formal QBRs:
- Know what your clients' business goals are: ask in discovery and revisit regularly
- Proactively bring ideas: "I noticed you're hiring three people. Want me to get ahead of the
  onboarding so it's seamless?"
- Be the person they call when they have a *business* problem, not just an IT problem
- Show up as an advisor, not a vendor

**In practice:**
- Check in monthly with key contacts: not about tickets, about their business
- When you spot a risk (end-of-life hardware, an approaching license renewal, compliance
  gap), bring it up before it becomes their problem
- Send a brief "what's coming" note quarterly instead of a formal review deck

## Pricing Philosophy

**Never publish fixed packages.** This is some of the strongest sales advice in MSP industry
literature:

- Published pricing tells prospects exactly what to expect before you've built trust or understood
  their situation
- It invites price shopping and commoditization
- Every client's environment is different: a "standard" package will either underprice complex
  clients or overprice simple ones

**Form-fit pricing instead:**
1. Run discovery to understand their full environment (devices, users, servers, network, needs)
2. Build a custom quote based on what they actually need
3. Present the quote in person or on a call. Never email it and wait
4. Own the conversation: "Here's what we'd recommend, and here's why"

**Pricing conversation openers:**
- "Before I give you any numbers, I want to make sure I'm quoting the right thing for your
  situation. Can I ask a few questions?"
- "Our pricing is based on your actual setup, not a package. That way you only pay for what
  you need."

## The Sales Funnel for SMBs

MSP sales to SMBs move faster than enterprise once a real buying trigger exists: a
referral-sourced or incident-driven deal can close in 1-4 weeks. Cold-sourced deals are a
different animal; most take months, and the marketing references' 6-18 month figure is the
honest planning number for outreach that starts cold. Both are true; the difference is where
the lead came from and whether something just broke.

**What accelerates deals:**
- A recent IT incident (ransomware, data loss, key employee left who handled IT)
- Compliance pressure (cyber insurance renewal, HIPAA audit, PCI requirement)
- Rapid business growth ("we're hiring and our setup can't keep up")
- Current IT provider failing them (slow response, gone out of business, retired)

**What stalls deals:**
- No immediate pain ("things are fine right now")
- Decision-maker not in the room
- Competing priorities (budget freeze, busy season)

**Your job:** Create urgency by helping prospects see risks they haven't quantified yet.
